Lesson 1

Introduction to Complex Numbers

A complex number is an element formed by combining a real quantity and an imaginary quantity. It extends the one-dimensional real number line to a complete two-dimensional mathematical field where algebraic equations such as $x^2 + 1 = 0$ possess complete solutions.

$$\mathbb{C} = \{z = a + bi \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R}, \ i^2 = -1\}$$
Worked Example

Consider $z = 3 + 4i$. Here, the real coefficient is $3$ and the imaginary coefficient is $4$. The number can also be represented as an ordered pair of real coordinates $(3, 4)$ on the Cartesian Argand plane.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Confusing the imaginary unit $i$ with a variable. In complex analysis, $i$ is not an unknown; it is a rigorously defined mathematical constant satisfying $i^2 = -1$.

Lesson 2

The Imaginary Unit i

The imaginary unit, denoted by $i$ (or $j$ in electrical engineering), is defined as the solution to the equation $x^2 = -1$. Integer powers of $i$ exhibit an exact cyclical repetition with a period of 4.

$$i^0 = 1, \quad i^1 = i, \quad i^2 = -1, \quad i^3 = -i, \quad i^{4k + r} = i^r \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z})$$
Worked Example: Simplifying $i^{105}$

Divide exponent 105 by 4: $105 = 4 \times 26 + 1$.
Therefore, $i^{105} = (i^4)^{26} \times i^1 = 1^{26} \times i = i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Applying $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ when both numbers are negative. That identity holds only when at least one radicand is non-negative. $\sqrt{-1} \times \sqrt{-1} = i \times i = -1 \neq \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{1} = 1$.

Lesson 3

Real and Imaginary Parts

Every complex number $z = a + bi$ has two distinct real components: the real part, denoted $\text{Re}(z) = a$, and the imaginary part, denoted $\text{Im}(z) = b$. Note that both $a$ and $b$ are strictly real numbers.

$$z = a + bi \implies \text{Re}(z) = a \in \mathbb{R}, \quad \text{Im}(z) = b \in \mathbb{R}$$
Worked Example

For $z = -5 + 7i$:
$\text{Re}(z) = -5$
$\text{Im}(z) = 7$ (note that the imaginary part is $7$, NOT $7i$).

⚠️ Common Mistake: Writing $\text{Im}(z) = 7i$. By definition, the imaginary part is the real scalar multiplying $i$.

Lesson 4

Equality of Complex Numbers

Two complex numbers are mathematically equal if and only if their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal. There is no natural total order on $\mathbb{C}$ (inequalities like $z_1 < z_2$ have no meaning).

$$a + bi = c + di \iff a = c \quad \text{and} \quad b = d$$
Worked Example: Solve $(2x - 1) + 3iy = 5 + 9i$

Equating real parts: $2x - 1 = 5 \implies 2x = 6 \implies x = 3$.
Equating imaginary parts: $3y = 9 \implies y = 3$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Trying to state that $2 + 3i > 1 + 4i$. The complex field cannot be given an ordered field structure compatible with arithmetic.

Lesson 5

Addition and Subtraction

Addition and subtraction of complex numbers operate component-wise. Combine the real components together, and separately combine the imaginary components together.

$$(a + bi) \pm (c + di) = (a \pm c) + (b \pm d)i$$
Worked Example

$(4 - 7i) - (-2 + 3i) = [4 - (-2)] + [-7 - 3]i = (4 + 2) + (-10)i = 6 - 10i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Forgetting to distribute the minus sign to both the real and imaginary parts of the subtrahend.

Lesson 6

Multiplication

Multiplication applies the algebraic distributive property (FOIL) combined with the substitution $i^2 = -1$.

$$(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$$
Worked Example: $(3 + 4i)(2 - i)$

Step 1: Distribute: $3(2) + 3(-i) + 4i(2) + 4i(-i) = 6 - 3i + 8i - 4i^2$
Step 2: Replace $i^2 = -1$: $6 + 5i - 4(-1) = 6 + 5i + 4 = 10 + 5i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Multiplying only the corresponding terms $(a \times c) + (b \times d)i$. Always multiply all four cross-terms!

Lesson 7

Division Using Conjugates

To divide by a nonzero complex number $c + di$, multiply both the numerator and the denominator by the complex conjugate of the denominator, $c - di$. This converts the denominator into a strictly positive real number $c^2 + d^2$.

$$\frac{a + bi}{c + di} = \frac{(a + bi)(c - di)}{(c + di)(c - di)} = \frac{(ac + bd) + (bc - ad)i}{c^2 + d^2}$$
Worked Example: $\frac{3 + 4i}{1 - 2i}$

Conjugate of denominator: $1 + 2i$.
$\frac{(3 + 4i)(1 + 2i)}{(1 - 2i)(1 + 2i)} = \frac{3 + 6i + 4i + 8i^2}{1^2 - (2i)^2} = \frac{3 + 10i - 8}{1 + 4} = \frac{-5 + 10i}{5} = -1 + 2i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Dividing by zero. A complex denominator is zero if and only if both its real and imaginary parts are zero.

Lesson 8

Complex Conjugates

The complex conjugate of $z = a + bi$, written $\bar{z}$ or $z^*$, is formed by reversing the sign of the imaginary component: $\bar{z} = a - bi$. Geometrically, this represents a reflection across the horizontal real axis.

$$z = a + bi \implies \bar{z} = a - bi, \quad z + \bar{z} = 2\text{Re}(z), \quad z - \bar{z} = 2i\text{Im}(z), \quad z\bar{z} = |z|^2$$
Worked Example

If $z = 3 - 4i$, then $\bar{z} = 3 + 4i$.
Identity check: $z\bar{z} = (3 - 4i)(3 + 4i) = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Inverting the sign of the real part. The real part remains unchanged: $\overline{a + bi} = a - bi$, not $-a - bi$.

Lesson 9

Modulus (Absolute Value)

The modulus or absolute value $|z|$ of a complex number $z = a + bi$ measures the Euclidean distance from the origin $(0, 0)$ to the point $(a, b)$ in the complex plane. It is always a non-negative real scalar.

$$|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{z \bar{z}}, \quad |z_1 z_2| = |z_1| |z_2|, \quad \left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$$
Worked Example: Calculate $|3 - 4i|$

$|3 - 4i| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Including $i$ inside the square: writing $\sqrt{a^2 + (bi)^2} = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$. The correct formula uses the square of the real coefficient $b^2$.

Lesson 10

Argument & Principal Angle

The argument $\arg(z)$ of a nonzero complex number is the counter-clockwise angle from the positive real axis to the vector representing $z$. The principal argument $\text{Arg}(z)$ is restricted to the interval $(-\pi, \pi]$ (or $(-180^\circ, 180^\circ]$).

$$\text{Arg}(z) = \text{atan2}(b, a) \in (-\pi, \pi], \quad \arg(z) = \text{Arg}(z) + 2k\pi \ (k \in \mathbb{Z})$$
Worked Example: Find $\text{Arg}(-1 + i)$

The point $(-1, 1)$ lies in Quadrant II.
$\theta = \arctan\left(\frac{1}{-1}\right) + \pi = -\frac{\pi}{4} + \pi = \frac{3\pi}{4} \ (135^\circ)$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: The argument of the zero complex number $z = 0$ is undefined, because the origin has no well-defined direction angle.

Lesson 11

Rectangular Representation

The rectangular (or Cartesian) form expresses a complex number as an algebraic sum of horizontal and vertical coordinates $z = a + bi$.

$$z = a + bi \longleftrightarrow (a, b) \in \mathbb{R}^2$$
Worked Example

Purely real numbers have $b = 0$, lying directly along the horizontal axis: $z = 5 + 0i = 5$. Purely imaginary numbers have $a = 0$, lying directly along the vertical axis: $z = 0 - 3i = -3i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Omitting the negative sign when formatting negative imaginary parts, e.g. writing $3 + -4i$ instead of $3 - 4i$.

Lesson 12

Polar Representation

Polar form characterizes a complex number by its magnitude (modulus $r$) and direction (argument $\theta$). In trigonometric notation, $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$.

$$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\text{ cis}(\theta), \quad r = |z| \ge 0, \quad \theta = \text{Arg}(z)$$
Worked Example: Convert $1 + i$ to Polar

$r = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$.
$\theta = \arctan(1/1) = \frac{\pi}{4} \ (45^\circ)$.
Polar form: $\sqrt{2}\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Using a negative value for the radial distance $r$. In standard polar form, $r \ge 0$.

Lesson 13

Exponential Form & Euler's Formula

Euler's famous formula connects trigonometry and complex exponentials: $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. Any complex number can therefore be written in compact exponential form as $z = r e^{i\theta}$.

$$z = r e^{i\theta}, \quad e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0 \quad (\text{Euler's Identity})$$
Worked Example: Multiplication in Exponential Form

If $z_1 = 2e^{i\pi/3}$ and $z_2 = 3e^{i\pi/6}$, their product is simply:
$z_1 z_2 = (2 \times 3) e^{i(\pi/3 + \pi/6)} = 6 e^{i\pi/2} = 6(0 + 1i) = 6i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Forgetting that $\theta$ in $e^{i\theta}$ must strictly be measured in radians, never degrees.

Lesson 14

Powers of Complex Numbers

Raising a complex number to an integer power $n$ scales its modulus by the $n$-th power and multiplies its argument by $n$.

$$(r e^{i\theta})^n = r^n e^{i n \theta}$$
Worked Example: Evaluate $(1 + i)^8$

Polar form: $1 + i = \sqrt{2} e^{i\pi/4}$.
$(1 + i)^8 = (\sqrt{2})^8 e^{i(8 \times \pi/4)} = 16 e^{i 2\pi} = 16(1 + 0i) = 16$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Expanding large powers algebraically with Pascal's triangle when polar form solves it in one line!

Lesson 15

De Moivre's Theorem

For any real number $\theta$ and any integer $n$, De Moivre's theorem provides a direct way to compute powers and trigonometric identities.

$$[\cos\theta + i\sin\theta]^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)$$
Worked Example

$[\cos(30^\circ) + i\sin(30^\circ)]^3 = \cos(90^\circ) + i\sin(90^\circ) = 0 + 1i = i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Applying the theorem directly to $r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ without raising the modulus $r$ to the power $n$. The full expression is $r^n(\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta)$.

Lesson 16

Square Roots and N-th Roots

Every nonzero complex number $z = r e^{i\theta}$ has exactly $n$ distinct $n$-th roots, distributed symmetrically around a circle of radius $r^{1/n}$ with equal angular separation $\frac{2\pi}{n}$.

$$w_k = r^{1/n} \left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right)\right], \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$$
Worked Example: Square roots of $-4$

$-4$ has $r = 4, \theta = \pi$.
$k = 0 \implies w_0 = \sqrt{4} e^{i\pi/2} = 2i$
$k = 1 \implies w_1 = \sqrt{4} e^{i(\pi + 2\pi)/2} = 2e^{i 3\pi/2} = -2i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Believing a nonzero complex number has only one square root. Every nonzero complex number has exactly two opposite square roots $\pm w_0$.

Lesson 17

Roots of Unity

The $n$-th roots of unity are the $n$ distinct complex solutions to the algebraic equation $z^n = 1$. They form the vertices of a regular $n$-gon inscribed in the unit circle centered at the origin.

$$\omega_k = e^{i\frac{2k\pi}{n}} = \cos\left(\frac{2k\pi}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{2k\pi}{n}\right), \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$$
Worked Example: Cube Roots of Unity ($n = 3$)

$\omega_0 = e^0 = 1$
$\omega_1 = e^{i 2\pi/3} = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i$
$\omega_2 = e^{i 4\pi/3} = -\frac{1}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i$
Note the fundamental property: $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Forgetting that the sum of all $n$-th roots of unity is identically zero for any $n > 1$.

Lesson 18

Reciprocal & Multiplicative Inverse

The multiplicative inverse $z^{-1}$ of any nonzero complex number $z = a + bi$ satisfies $z \cdot z^{-1} = 1$. It can be computed directly by dividing the conjugate $\bar{z}$ by the squared modulus $|z|^2$.

$$z^{-1} = \frac{1}{z} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2} = \frac{a - bi}{a^2 + b^2} \quad (z \neq 0)$$
Worked Example: Find $(3 + 4i)^{-1}$

$|z|^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25$. Conjugate $\bar{z} = 3 - 4i$.
$z^{-1} = \frac{3 - 4i}{25} = \frac{3}{25} - \frac{4}{25}i = 0.12 - 0.16i$.
Verification: $(3 + 4i)(0.12 - 0.16i) = 0.36 + 0.64 + i(-0.48 + 0.48) = 1 + 0i = 1$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Zero has no multiplicative inverse. $1/0$ is undefined in the complex field $\mathbb{C}$.

Lesson 19

Complex Linear & Quadratic Equations

Linear equations $az + b = c$ and quadratic equations $az^2 + bz + c = 0$ with complex coefficients can be solved using algebraic isolation and the complex quadratic formula with square roots of the complex discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$.

$$z = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{\Delta}}{2a}, \quad \Delta = b^2 - 4ac$$
Worked Example: Solve $z^2 - 2z + 5 = 0$

Coefficients: $a = 1, b = -2, c = 5$.
Discriminant: $\Delta = (-2)^2 - 4(1)(5) = 4 - 20 = -16$.
$\sqrt{\Delta} = \sqrt{-16} = \pm 4i$.
Roots: $z = \frac{-(-2) \pm 4i}{2} = \frac{2 \pm 4i}{2} = 1 \pm 2i$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Assuming a quadratic equation with complex coefficients always produces conjugate pair roots. Conjugate pair roots occur only when the polynomial's coefficients are strictly real!

Lesson 20

Geometric Interpretation on the Complex Plane

The complex plane (Argand diagram) maps complex numbers to 2D vector geometry. Addition corresponds to the parallelogram law of vector addition, conjugation corresponds to reflection across the real axis, and multiplication by $r e^{i\theta}$ scales the vector by $r$ and rotates it counter-clockwise by $\theta$.

$$\text{Multiplication by } i \longleftrightarrow \text{Counter-Clockwise Rotation by } 90^\circ \ (\pi/2 \text{ rad})$$
Worked Example

Multiply $z = 2 + 1i$ by $i$:
$i(2 + i) = 2i + i^2 = -1 + 2i$.
Notice that vector $(2, 1)$ has rotated exactly $90^\circ$ counter-clockwise to $(-1, 2)$ while preserving its modulus $\sqrt{5}$.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Viewing complex numbers merely as abstract algebraic symbols rather than dynamic geometric transformations in two dimensions.
